## Glossary

**Stomata** — Tiny leaf pores that let carbon dioxide enter but also allow water vapor to escape. [cue-3, cue-10]

**Carbon fixation** — The journey of a carbon atom from carbon dioxide into an organic molecule inside the plant, via the Calvin cycle. [cue-6, cue-8]

**G3P** — A small three-carbon molecule produced by the Calvin cycle and used to build other organic molecules. [cue-15]

## Source transcript

cue-1 (00:00–00:24): Look at a tree outside your window. Its trunk feels solid, heavy, and very different from the air around it. Where did that material come from? A common first answer is the soil. Roots do take up water and minerals, but the carbon in a tree's organic molecules comes mainly from carbon dioxide in the air. Photosynthesis is the process that brings that carbon into the living world.

cue-2 (00:24–00:51): We need to separate two questions: where the atoms come from, and where the energy comes from. Carbon dioxide and water provide atoms. Light supplies energy. Light is not an ingredient that turns into carbon atoms. During photosynthesis, the plant uses energy to rearrange matter into molecules that can store chemical energy. Keeping matter and energy separate makes the whole process much easier to understand.

cue-3 (00:51–01:20): A leaf brings together the ingredients and structures needed for photosynthesis. Tiny pores, called stomata, allow carbon dioxide to enter. Water arrives through the plant's transport tissues. Inside leaf cells, chloroplasts contain the machinery that captures light and builds organic molecules. A leaf is not simply a green solar panel. It is also a place where gases move, water travels, and many connected chemical reactions take place.

cue-4 (01:20–01:45): Why do many leaves look green? Chlorophyll absorbs some wavelengths of light more strongly than others, especially in the red and blue regions. A greater proportion of green light is reflected or transmitted, so green is the color that often reaches our eyes. Saying that plants are green because they absorb only green light reverses the explanation. Leaf color is a clue about which light is not absorbed as strongly.

cue-5 (01:45–02:11): None of this means that soil is unimportant. Plants need minerals, water, and a suitable environment for their roots. Those resources support growth, but they play different roles from carbon dioxide. When we say that much of a plant's dry organic material comes from the air, we are following carbon atoms. We are not saying that a plant can live without roots, water, or mineral nutrients.

cue-6 (02:11–02:38): Before moving on, imagine following a single carbon atom. It begins in a carbon dioxide molecule outside the leaf. It passes through a stoma and eventually becomes part of an organic molecule inside the plant. That journey is carbon fixation. Next, we will separate carbon's journey from another important story: where the oxygen released by the leaf comes from. The two stories are connected, but they are not the same.

cue-7 (02:38–03:07): Here is the question that catches many people: where does the oxygen gas released during photosynthesis come from? It comes from water. During the light-dependent reactions, water molecules are split, providing electrons and protons and releasing oxygen. Carbon dioxide contains oxygen atoms too, which makes it a tempting answer. But the oxygen gas released by these reactions is produced from water, not by simply splitting carbon dioxide.

cue-8 (03:07–03:37): Carbon dioxide has a different role. Its carbon is incorporated into organic molecules through the Calvin cycle. That does not mean carbon dioxide is unimportant to photosynthesis; it is essential to building the carbon skeletons of those molecules. The useful distinction is this: water supplies the electrons used in the light reactions, and carbon dioxide supplies the carbon used in carbon fixation. The ingredients participate in different parts of the process.

cue-9 (03:37–04:04): A summary equation compresses many reactions into a single line. It tells us which substances are consumed and produced overall, but it does not show the route taken by each atom. If you want to understand where released oxygen comes from, you must follow the reactions that split water. Looking only at the oxygen symbols in carbon dioxide can lead you to the wrong conclusion. Mechanism matters more than a quick visual guess.

cue-10 (04:04–04:31): Stomata create a trade-off. Opening them allows carbon dioxide to diffuse into the leaf, but water vapor can also escape. Closing them can conserve water while limiting the entry of carbon dioxide. This helps explain why water availability and gas exchange are connected in a living plant. Photosynthesis does not happen in isolation from the environment; the leaf has to manage resources while carrying out its chemistry.

cue-11 (04:31–05:01): Try explaining the distinction without using a memorized equation. Water is split in the light-dependent reactions, and oxygen is released. Carbon from carbon dioxide is incorporated into organic molecules. Light provides the energy that supports the process. If those sentences remain clear in your mind, you can answer several different questions instead of remembering only one pattern of words. Understanding is being able to keep the roles straight when the question changes.

cue-12 (05:01–05:31): Now move inside a chloroplast. It contains membranes organized into structures called thylakoids, surrounded by a fluid region called the stroma. The light-dependent reactions take place in the thylakoid membranes. The Calvin cycle takes place in the stroma. These locations help us organize the two connected jobs: converting light energy into useful chemical forms, and using those forms to support the incorporation of carbon into organic molecules.

cue-13 (05:31–06:01): The light reactions produce ATP and NADPH. These are useful chemical products that connect the light reactions to the Calvin cycle. ATP provides energy for reactions, while NADPH provides reducing power. You do not need to treat them as tiny packets of sunlight. They are molecules with specific roles in a connected chemical system. The Calvin cycle uses their contributions as it processes carbon dioxide into organic material.

cue-14 (06:01–06:29): You may hear the Calvin cycle called light-independent reactions. This means that its reactions do not directly capture photons. It does not mean the process is a separate night shift that only runs in darkness. The cycle depends on products of the light reactions, and its activity is coordinated with the state of the chloroplast. Be careful when a label sounds like an everyday instruction; scientific names often carry a narrower meaning.

cue-15 (06:29–06:57): The Calvin cycle produces a small three-carbon molecule called G3P that can be used to build other organic molecules. Glucose is a convenient familiar name in simplified accounts, but it is not helpful to imagine a whole glucose molecule appearing as the single direct product of every turn. Plants use the products of photosynthesis in many ways, including making sugars, storing material, and building structures needed for growth.

cue-16 (06:57–07:27): The next time you look at a leaf, ask three questions. Where does its carbon come from? Carbon dioxide in the air. Where does the released oxygen come from? Water split in the light reactions. What does light contribute? Energy that supports the chemistry. Those three questions turn a green object into a story about matter and energy. If you can explain each answer in a different way, you are beginning to connect the mechanism rather than just repeat the vocabulary.
